\title{From clusters to curves: A model-based alternative to
cluster-based permutation tests for the analysis of time-resolved data}


\shorttitle{Bayesian modelling of M/EEG data}


\usepackage{etoolbox}









\authorsnames[{1},{2}]{Ladislas Nalborczyk,Paul Bürkner}







\authorsaffiliations{
{Aix Marseille Univ, CNRS, LPL},{TU Dortmund University, Department of

\leftheader{Nalborczyk and Bürkner}



\abstract{Time-resolved electrophysiological measurements such as those
obtained through magneto- and electroencephalography (M/EEG) offer a
unique window onto the neural activity underlying cognitive processes.
Researchers are often interested in determining whether and when these
signals differ across experimental conditions or participant groups. The
conventional approach involves mass univariate statistical testing
across time and space followed by corrections for multiple comparisons
or some form of cluster-based inference. While effective for controlling
error rates at the cluster-level, cluster-based inference comes with a
significant limitation: by shifting the focus of inference from
individual time points to clusters, it prevents drawing conclusions
about the precise onset or offset of observed effects. Here, we present
a \emph{model-based} alternative for analysing M/EEG time series, such
as event-related potentials or time-resolved decoding accuracy. Our
approach leverages Bayesian generalised additive multilevel models,
providing posterior odds at each time point, while naturally accounting
for between-participant variability. Using simulated data with known
ground truth, we show that the proposed approach consistently provides
accurate recovery of the overall temporal extent and onset of neural
effects across a range of signal-to-noise ratios, although offset
localisation is comparatively more variable. In an empirical MEG
decoding dataset, the approach also yields the highest split-half
temporal overlap among the methods considered and highly stable temporal
segmentation across repeated participant splits. Together, these results
illustrate the potential of model-based inference as a complementary
approach to conventional cluster-based procedures when temporally
localised inference and explicit modelling of between-participant
variability are scientifically important. We provide an open-source R
package implementing the method and describe how it can be integrated
into M/EEG analysis pipelines using MNE-Python. }

\keywords{EEG, MEG, cluster-based permutation tests, generalised
additive models, mixed-effects models, multilevel models, Bayesian
statistics, brms}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

\setcounter{page}{2}

\subsection{Problem statement}\label{problem-statement}

Understanding the temporal dynamics of cognitive processes requires
methods that can capture fast-changing neural activity with high
temporal resolution. Magnetoencephalography and electroencephalography
(M/EEG) are two such methods, widely used in cognitive neuroscience for
their ability to track brain activity at the millisecond scale. These
techniques provide rich time series data that reflect how neural
responses unfold in response to stimuli or tasks. A central goal in many
M/EEG studies is to determine whether, when, and where neural responses
differ across experimental conditions or groups.

The conventional approach for addressing such questions relies on
mass-univariate statistical testing across time and/or sensors, followed
by corrections for multiple comparisons to maintain nominal error rates
(e.g., FWER or FDR). Cluster-based permutation inference is the most
common strategy in M/EEG research and is implemented as a recommended
workflow in widely used software ecosystems (e.g., \texttt{EEGLAB},
\citeproc{ref-delorme2004}{Delorme \& Makeig, 2004};
\texttt{MNE-Python}, \citeproc{ref-gramfort2013}{Gramfort, 2013}). While
effective for controlling error rates at the cluster level,
cluster-based permutation tests also impose important constraints on
what can be concluded. By design, they shift inference away from
individual samples (time points, sensors, voxels) toward clusters formed
from contiguous supra-threshold test statistics, which limits the
interpretability of spatial and temporal localisation
(\citeproc{ref-maris2007}{Maris \& Oostenveld, 2007};
\citeproc{ref-sassenhagen2019}{Sassenhagen \& Draschkow, 2019}).
Importantly, the limitations of cluster-based inference extend beyond
temporal imprecision in onset/offset estimation. Cluster-based outcomes
can be sensitive to analysis choices (e.g., cluster-forming threshold or
TFCE settings, significance threshold, test statistic, and crucially,
the analysis window) and do not provide an explicit probabilistic model
for how effects evolve over time at the population level.

To address these limitations, we introduce a model-based framework for
time-resolved M/EEG outcomes based on Bayesian generalised additive
multilevel models. The core idea is to model the expected signal over
time as a smooth function, with hierarchical structure to represent
between-participant variability, and to quantify evidence for
above-baseline (or above-chance) effects directly in the posterior
distribution at the level of individual time points (and, by extension,
sensors/voxels). This approach regularises the mean time course via
smoothness priors and yields coherent uncertainty quantification for
time-localised quantities. Using simulated data with known ground truth
and an empirical MEG decoding dataset, we show how this framework
complements standard permutation-based approaches: it provides
interpretable, model-based uncertainty about when effects emerge and
subside, while making the role of modelling choices (e.g., basis
dimension) explicit and amenable to sensitivity analysis.

\subsection{Statistical errors and cluster-based
inference}\label{statistical-errors-and-cluster-based-inference}

The issues with multiple comparisons represent a common and
well-recognised danger in neuroimaging and M/EEG research, where the
collected data allow for a multitude of potential hypothesis tests and
is characterised by complex structures of spatiotemporal dependencies.
The probability of obtaining at least one false positive in an ensemble
(family) of \(m\) independent tests (i.e., the FWER) is computed as
\(1-\left(1-\alpha\right)^{m}\) (for \(m=10\) independent tests and
\(\alpha=0.05\), it is approximately equal to \(0.4\)). Different
methods exist to control the FWER, that is, to bring it back to
\(\alpha\). Most methods apply a simple correction to series of
\(p\)-values issued from univariate statistical tests (e.g., t-tests).
For instance, the Bonferroni correction sets the per-test significance
threshold to \(\alpha/m\), or equivalently multiplies each \(p\)-value
by \(m\). This procedure controls the FWER without requiring
independence among tests, but can be conservative because it does not
exploit their dependence structure. This is particularly relevant for
M/EEG data, in which neighbouring temporal and spatial samples are often
strongly correlated. Alternative procedures control the false discovery
rate (FDR), defined as the expected proportion of false discoveries
among rejected hypotheses (e.g., \citeproc{ref-benjamini1995}{Benjamini
\& Hochberg, 1995}; \citeproc{ref-benjamini2001}{Benjamini \& Yekutieli,
2001}). Although these procedures provide useful multiplicity control,
they do not explicitly model the spatial and temporal structure of M/EEG
signals.

A popular technique to account for spatiotemporal dependencies while
controlling the FWER is cluster-based inference
(\citeproc{ref-bullmore1999}{Bullmore et al., 1999};
\citeproc{ref-maris2007}{Maris \& Oostenveld, 2007}). A typical
cluster-based permutation procedure involves two successive steps (for
more details on cluster-based inference, see for instance
\citeproc{ref-frossard2022}{Frossard \& Renaud, 2022};
\citeproc{ref-maris2011}{Maris, 2011}; \citeproc{ref-maris2007}{Maris \&
Oostenveld, 2007}; \citeproc{ref-sassenhagen2019}{Sassenhagen \&
Draschkow, 2019}). First, clusters are defined as sets of contiguous
time points, sensors, voxels, etc, whose activity, summarised by some
test statistic (e.g., a \(t\)-value), exceeds a predefined threshold
(e.g., the 95th percentile of the parametric null distribution).
Clusters are then characterised by their height (i.e., maximal value),
extent (number of constituent elements), or some combination of both,
for instance by summing the statistics within a cluster, an approach
referred to as ``cluster mass'' (\citeproc{ref-maris2007}{Maris \&
Oostenveld, 2007}; \citeproc{ref-pernet2015}{Pernet et al., 2015}).
Then, the null hypothesis is tested by computing a \(p\)-value for each
identified cluster by comparing its mass with the null distribution of
cluster masses (obtained via permutation). A significant cluster
indicates that the cluster-level statistic is unlikely under the null
hypothesis; it does not imply that each constituent sample is
individually significant. As such, it would be incorrect to conclude,
for instance, that the time point of a significant cluster is the first
moment at which some conditions differ
(\citeproc{ref-frossard2022}{Frossard \& Renaud, 2022};
\citeproc{ref-sassenhagen2019}{Sassenhagen \& Draschkow, 2019}). Because
the inference is performed at the second step (i.e., once clusters have
been formed), no conclusion can be made about individual datapoints
(e.g., time points, sensors, etc). More recent procedures such as
ClusterDepth provide strong FWER-controlled inference at the level of
individual samples while retaining a cluster-based framework, although
their performance for precise onset localisation can remain limited.

As different cluster-forming thresholds lead to clusters with different
spatial or temporal extent, this initial threshold modulates the
sensitivity of the subsequent permutation test. The threshold-free
cluster enhancement (TFCE) method was introduced by S. Smith \& Nichols
(\citeproc{ref-smith2009}{2009}) to avoid the need to select a single
arbitrary cluster-forming threshold. Rather than forming clusters at one
threshold, TFCE evaluates each sample across a range of thresholds. At a
given threshold \(h\), the contribution to the TFCE score depends
jointly on the height of the threshold and on the extent \(e_t(h)\) of
the cluster containing sample \(t\). The TFCE score at time point \(t\)
can be written as:

\[
\text{TFCE(t)} = \int_{0}^{h_{t}} e_{t}(h)^{E} h^{H} \mathrm{d}h
\]

where \(h_{t}\) is the observed statistic at time point \(t\), and the
parameters \(E\) and \(H\) control the relative contributions of cluster
extent and height, respectively (typically \(E=0.5\) and \(H=2\)). In
practice, this integral is approximated numerically over a series of
thresholds. Statistical significance is then assessed by permutation:
for each permutation, the maximum TFCE statistic across the analysed
samples is retained to construct a null distribution, against which the
observed sample-wise TFCE scores are compared. This procedure can
therefore provide FWER-controlled inference at the sample level without
requiring a single cluster-forming threshold (e.g.,
\citeproc{ref-pernet2015}{Pernet et al., 2015};
\citeproc{ref-smith2009}{S. Smith \& Nichols, 2009}). Nevertheless,
simulation studies have shown that the temporal boundaries identified by
cluster-based procedures, including TFCE, can provide biased or
imprecise estimates of effect onset and offset
(\citeproc{ref-rousselet_using_2025}{Rousselet, 2025};
\citeproc{ref-sassenhagen2019}{Sassenhagen \& Draschkow, 2019}).

\subsection{Further limitations of cluster-based permutation
tests}\label{further-limitations-of-cluster-based-permutation-tests}

Although cluster-based permutation tests are widely used and often
appropriate for controlling error rates under a global null, they come
with several limitations that are especially relevant for time-resolved
M/EEG questions. First, temporal dependencies are not explicitly
modelled. Cluster-based inference does not specify a generative model
for temporal dependence in the measured signal. Instead, temporal
structure is summarised post-hoc by identifying clusters of adjacent
supra-threshold test statistics (e.g., t- or F-values). Second,
within-participant variability is typically not explicitly modelled. In
many workflows, data are averaged within participants (e.g., ERPs,
decoding time courses) prior to group inference. Although this reduces
data dimensionality, it discards information about within-participant
variability and differences in measurement precision across
participants. Consequently, participant-level estimates are typically
treated as equally reliable, potentially reducing statistical efficiency
and limiting the explicit modelling of different sources of uncertainty.
Third, participant-level clusters (when extracted) do not yield a
principled population model of timing variability. It is possible to
extract clusters per participant, but the resulting onset/offset
summaries do not by themselves quantify between-participant variability
unless an explicit second-stage model is specified. Fourth, results can
vary substantially with several analysis ``hyper-parameters''. These
include the cluster-forming threshold (or TFCE parameters), the
significance threshold and tail definition, the choice of test
statistic, the temporal/spatial analysis window, and preprocessing
decisions that affect temporal resolution (e.g., filtering, decimation).

Together, these considerations motivate a complementary approach in
which the temporal trajectory is itself part of the statistical model,
uncertainty can be propagated to time-localised quantities, and
between-participant variability can be represented explicitly. This idea
is not entirely new: regression-based and smooth-model approaches have
increasingly been used to estimate electrophysiological responses. We
therefore first review this work before introducing the GAM framework
used here.

\subsection{Previous work on modelling M/EEG
data}\label{previous-work-on-modelling-meeg-data}

M/EEG signals capture neural activity originating from various brain
regions and are often contaminated by artefacts unrelated to the
cognitive processes under investigation. Consequently, analysing M/EEG
data necessitates methods that can disentangle task-relevant neural
signals from extraneous ``noise.'' A widely adopted technique for this
purpose is the estimation of event-related potentials (ERPs), which are
stereotyped electrophysiological responses time-locked to specific
sensory, cognitive, or motor events. Typically, ERPs are derived by
averaging EEG or MEG epochs across multiple trials aligned to the event
of interest (e.g., stimulus onset), thereby enhancing the
signal-to-noise ratio by attenuating non-time-locked activity. However,
this averaging approach has notable limitations: it assumes consistent
latency and amplitude across trials and is primarily suited for simple
categorical designs. Such assumptions may not hold in more complex
experimental paradigms, potentially leading to suboptimal ERP
estimations (e.g., \citeproc{ref-smith2014a}{N. J. Smith \& Kutas,
2014a}).

To overcome the limitations of simple averaging, several model-based
approaches for estimating ERPs have been proposed. These methods are
motivated by the observation that traditional ERP averaging is
mathematically equivalent to fitting an intercept-only linear regression
model in a simple categorical design without overlapping events
(\citeproc{ref-smith2014a}{N. J. Smith \& Kutas, 2014a}). In contrast to
simple averaging, regression-based approaches to ERP estimation offer
substantially greater flexibility. Notably, they allow for the modelling
of both linear and nonlinear effects of continuous predictors, such as
word frequency or age (e.g., \citeproc{ref-smith2014a}{N. J. Smith \&
Kutas, 2014a}, \citeproc{ref-smith2014b}{2014b};
\citeproc{ref-tremblay2014}{Tremblay \& Newman, 2014}), and enable the
disentangling of overlapping cognitive processes (e.g.,
\citeproc{ref-ehinger_unfold_2019}{Ehinger \& Dimigen, 2019};
\citeproc{ref-skukies_brain_2024}{Skukies et al., 2024};
\citeproc{ref-skukies_modelling_2021}{Skukies \& Ehinger, 2021}). One
widely used implementation of this approach is provided by the LIMO EEG
toolbox (\citeproc{ref-pernet2011}{Pernet et al., 2011}), which follows
a multi-stage analysis pipeline. First, a separate regression model is
fit for each datapoint (e.g., each time point and electrode) at the
individual level to estimate ERP responses. This is followed by
group-level statistical analyses of the resulting regression
coefficients, often accompanied by corrections for multiple comparisons
or cluster-based inference (for recent applied examples, see
\citeproc{ref-dunagan2025}{Dunagan et al., 2025};
\citeproc{ref-wuxfcllhorst2025}{Wüllhorst et al., 2025}).

Although this framework allows for the inclusion of a wide range of
predictors (both continuous and categorical, linear and nonlinear) it
still has important limitations. First, fitting separate models for each
datapoint ignores the spatiotemporal dependencies inherent in M/EEG
data, potentially reducing statistical power and interpretability.
Second, the subsequent group-level analyses typically do not account for
hierarchical dependencies which could otherwise be addressed through
multilevel modelling. Finally, because the output of this procedure is
summarised by cluster-based inference, its conclusions remain subject to
the limitations discussed in the previous section.

Beyond modelling nonlinear effects of continuous predictors on ERP
amplitudes, GAMs have been employed to capture the temporal dynamics of
ERPs themselves, effectively modelling the shape of the waveform over
time (\citeproc{ref-abugaber2023}{Abugaber et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-baayen2018}{Baayen et al., 2018};
\citeproc{ref-meulman2015}{Meulman et al., 2015},
\citeproc{ref-meulman2023}{2023}). This approach allows for the
estimation of smooth, data-driven functions that characterise how neural
responses evolve over time, offering a flexible alternative to
traditional linear models. These developments show that regression
models can move M/EEG analysis beyond simple averaging and
sample-by-sample testing. GAMs provide a particularly natural extension
because they model the temporal trajectory itself as a smooth,
data-driven function.

\subsection{Generalised additive
models}\label{generalised-additive-models}

In generalised additive models, the functional relationship between the
predictors and the response variable is decomposed into a sum of
low-dimensional non-parametric functions. A typical GAM has the
following form:

\[
\begin{aligned} 
y_{i} &\sim \mathrm{EF}\left(\mu_{i}, \phi\right)\\
g\left(\mu_i\right) &= \underbrace{\mathbf{A}_{i} \gamma}_{\text{parametric part}} + \underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{J} f_{j}\left(x_{ij}\right)}_{\text{non-parametric part}}
\end{aligned}
\]

where \(y_{i} \sim \mathrm{EF}\left(\mu_{i}, \phi\right)\) denotes that
the observations \(y_{i}\) are distributed as some member of the
exponential family of distributions (e.g., Gaussian, Gamma, Beta,
Poisson) with mean \(\mu_{i}\) and scale parameter \(\phi\);
\(g(\cdot)\) is the link function, \(\mathbf{A}_{i}\) is the \(i\)th row
of a known parametric model matrix, \(\gamma\) is a vector of parameters
for the parametric terms (to be estimated), \(f_{j}\) is a smooth
function of covariate \(x_{j}\) (to be estimated as well). The smooth
functions \(f_{j}\) are represented in the model as a weighted sum of
\(K\) simpler, basis functions:

\[
f_{j}\left(x_{i j}\right) = \sum_{k=1}^K \beta_{jk} b_{jk}\left(x_{ij}\right)
\]

where \(\beta_{jk}\) is the weight (coefficient) associated with the
\(k\)th basis function \(b_{jk}()\) evaluated at the covariate value
\(x_{ij}\) for the \(j\)th smooth function \(f_{j}\). To clarify the
terminology at this point: \emph{splines} are functions composed of
simpler functions. These simpler functions are called \emph{basis
functions} (e.g., cubic polynomial, thin-plate) and the set of basis
functions is called a \emph{basis}. Each basis function is weighted by
its coefficient and the resultant spline is the sum of these weighted
basis functions (Figure~\ref{fig-intro-gam}\ignorespaces A). Spline
coefficients are penalised (usually through the square of the smooth
functions' second derivative) in a way that can be interpreted, in
Bayesian terms, as a prior on the ``wiggliness'' of the function
(\citeproc{ref-miller2025}{Miller, 2025}; \citeproc{ref-wood2017}{Wood,
2017}). In other words, more complex (wiggly) basis functions are
automatically penalised.

A detailed treatment of the technical underpinnings of GAMs is beyond
the scope of this article (see reference books such as
\citeproc{ref-hastie2017}{Hastie \& Tibshirani, 2017};
\citeproc{ref-wood2017}{Wood, 2017}). However, it is worth emphasising
that GAMs have been successfully applied to a wide range of time series
data across the cognitive sciences, including pupillometry (e.g.,
\citeproc{ref-vanrij2019}{Rij et al., 2019}), articulography (e.g.,
\citeproc{ref-wieling2018}{Wieling, 2018}), speech formant dynamics
(e.g., \citeproc{ref-suxf3skuthy2021}{Sóskuthy, 2021}), neuroimaging
data (e.g., \citeproc{ref-dinga2021}{Dinga et al., 2021}), and
event-related potentials (e.g., \citeproc{ref-abugaber2023}{Abugaber et
al., 2023}; \citeproc{ref-baayen2018}{Baayen et al., 2018};
\citeproc{ref-meulman2015}{Meulman et al., 2015},
\citeproc{ref-meulman2023}{2023}). Their appeal for modelling M/EEG data
lies in their ability to flexibly capture the complex shape of ERP
waveforms without overfitting, through the use of smooth functions
constrained by penalisation. Recent extensions, such as distributional
GAMs (\citeproc{ref-rigby2005}{Rigby \& Stasinopoulos, 2005};
\citeproc{ref-umlauf2018}{Umlauf et al., 2018}), allow researchers to
model not only the mean structure but also the variance (or scale) and
other distributional properties as functions of predictors, a feature
that has proven useful in modelling neuroimaging data (e.g.,
\citeproc{ref-dinga2021}{Dinga et al., 2021}). Moreover, hierarchical or
multilevel GAMs (\citeproc{ref-pedersen_hierarchical_2019}{E. J.
Pedersen et al., 2019}) provide a principled way to account for the
nested structure of M/EEG data (e.g., trials within participants),
enabling the inclusion of varying intercepts, slopes, and smoothers (as
illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig-intro-gam}\ignorespaces C-D). This
approach mitigates the risk of overfitting and reduces the influence of
outliers on smooth estimates (\citeproc{ref-baayen2020}{Baayen \& Linke,
2020}; \citeproc{ref-meulman2023}{Meulman et al., 2023}).

\subsection{Objectives}\label{objectives}

Here, we develop and evaluate a model-based framework for temporally
localised inference in M/EEG data using Bayesian generalised additive
multilevel models implemented in \texttt{brms}
(\citeproc{ref-brms2017}{Bürkner, 2017}, \citeproc{ref-brms2018}{2018}).
We first benchmark the complete analysis workflow against commonly used
point-wise, multiplicity-corrected, cluster-based, and change point
procedures using simulated EEG data with known temporal ground truth
across four SNR levels. We then apply the same framework to empirical
MEG decoding data and evaluate the stability of temporal detections
using repeated split-half resampling. Our goal is not to isolate the
independent contribution of smoothing, Bayesian inference, or multilevel
modelling, but to assess the operating characteristics of the complete
model-based workflow.

\section{Benchmarking with known ground
truth}\label{benchmarking-with-known-ground-truth}

\subsection{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsubsection{M/EEG data simulation}\label{meeg-data-simulation}

To assess the accuracy of group-level temporal localisation with our
proposed method, we simulated EEG data with known ground-truth temporal
effects. Following the general approach of Sassenhagen \& Draschkow
(\citeproc{ref-sassenhagen2019}{2019}) and Rousselet
(\citeproc{ref-rousselet_using_2025}{2025}), each simulated dataset
comprised two experimental conditions: a reference condition containing
noise only and a target condition containing the same background
activity plus a time-localised signal. Each dataset contained 20
participants, with 50 trials per participant and condition, sampled at
500 Hz over a 0.5-s epoch. The simulation framework allowed the temporal
structure of the true effect to be specified explicitly. The true effect
extended from 160 to 340 ms.

To introduce participant-level heterogeneity, the amplitude of the true
effect was scaled separately for each participant, with scaling
coefficients drawn from a truncated normal distribution with mean 1 and
standard deviation 0.25. Participant-level noise magnitude was also
varied, using a truncated normal distribution with mean 1 and standard
deviation 0.5. Signal strength was manipulated across four
signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) levels (\(0.25, 0.5, 1, 2\)). SNR scaling
was referenced to the 0-100 ms baseline period, which was also used
subsequently to derive the baseline-dependent thresholds for the BGAM
analyses. Figure~\ref{fig-eeg} illustrates one simulated dataset.

\subsubsection{Model description and model
fitting}\label{model-description-and-model-fitting}

We first fitted a Bayesian generalised additive model (BGAM) using the
\texttt{brms} package (\citeproc{ref-brms2017}{Bürkner, 2017},
\citeproc{ref-brms2018}{2018}), which we subsequently extended to a
Bayesian generalised additive multilevel model (BGAMM) to account for
between-participant variability (for an introduction to Bayesian
multilevel modelling in \texttt{brms}, see
\citeproc{ref-nalborczyk2019}{Nalborczyk et al., 2019}). The BGAMM
included population-level (i.e., ``fixed'') intercepts, slopes, and
smooth effects of time, as well as participant-level (i.e., ``random'')
varying intercepts, slopes, and smooths. Although this model can be
fitted directly to single-trial data, we adopted a computationally more
efficient formulation based on participant-level summary statistics
(means and standard errors), analogous to a Bayesian meta-analytic
model. Specifically, the reported standard errors were incorporated into
the likelihood to account for differences in measurement precision
across observations, while an additional residual variance parameter was
estimated to capture variability not explained by these standard errors
or the model predictors.

Posterior distributions were approximated using Markov chain Monte Carlo
(MCMC) sampling, with four independent chains of 5,000 iterations each,
including 1,000 warmup iterations per chain. This yielded a total of
16,000 post-warmup draws for posterior inference. Convergence was
assessed through visual inspection of trace plots and the
rank-normalised split-\(\hat{R}\) diagnostic
(\citeproc{ref-gabry2019}{Gabry et al., 2019};
\citeproc{ref-gelman2020}{Gelman et al., 2020};
\citeproc{ref-vehtari2021}{Vehtari et al., 2021a}).

Figure~\ref{fig-bgam-procedure} illustrates the BGAMM predictions
alongside the simulated data. The model adequately captures the
underlying temporal dynamics of both conditions (see additional
posterior predictive checks in \hyperref[apx-basis]{Appendix~C}). Panel
A shows the posterior expected responses for each condition, while Panel
B shows the estimated time-varying condition contrast \(\beta(t)\). The
shaded regions represent point-wise 95\% credible intervals, which
quantify posterior uncertainty at each time point, conditional on the
model, priors, and observed data.

To identify periods of substantial condition differences, we computed
the posterior probability that the time-varying condition contrast,
\(\beta(t)\), exceeds a smallest effect size of interest (SESOI),
denoted \(\delta\). We operationally defined this SESOI from the
pre-effect baseline interval (0-0.1 s) as the maximum absolute bound of
the point-wise 95\% credible intervals for the condition contrast,
considering both their lower (2.5th percentile) and upper (97.5th
percentile) bounds across all baseline time points. This definition
ensures that the resulting symmetric region of practical equivalence
{[}ROPE; Kruschke \& Liddell (\citeproc{ref-kruschke2017}{2017}){]},
\([-\delta, \delta]\), encompasses the entire point-wise 95\% credible
interval at every baseline time point (orange shading in Panel B). This
baseline-derived threshold provides a model-based reference against
which post-onset effects can be evaluated, accounting for both the
magnitude and posterior uncertainty of estimated condition differences
during a period when no effect is expected.

At each time point, we computed the posterior probability of exceeding
this threshold, \(p(t) = \Pr(\beta(t) > \delta \mid \text{data})\), and
converted it into posterior odds, \(p(t)/(1-p(t))\). These odds quantify
the relative posterior support for the condition contrast exceeding
versus not exceeding the SESOI. For example, posterior odds of 3
indicate that an effect exceeding \(\delta\) is three times as probable
as one not exceeding it, conditional on the model and observed data. As
illustrated in Panel C, contiguous time points with posterior odds
exceeding a predefined threshold (3 in this example) were grouped into
temporal clusters, providing estimates of the onset and offset of
periods with strong posterior evidence for an effect. The operating
characteristics of this procedure were evaluated in the simulation
study. Because inference is based on posterior contrasts, the approach
can be extended to a broad range of model structures and experimental
designs.

In addition to identifying effect intervals from the posterior-odds
trajectory, we quantified uncertainty in their temporal boundaries
directly. For each joint posterior draw of the time-varying condition
contrast, we identified the earliest and latest time points at which
\(\beta(t)\) exceeded the same SESOI, \(\delta\). Repeating this
procedure across posterior draws yielded distributions of effect onset
and offset (illustrated near the bottom of Panel C). Draws without any
threshold exceedance were retained when estimating the posterior
probability of an effect being present but excluded from the conditional
distributions of onset and offset. This provides complementary
information to the posterior-odds-based approach: whereas the latter
identifies periods with sufficiently strong posterior evidence for an
effect, the former characterises posterior uncertainty about the timing
of its boundaries.

\subsubsection{Comparing the onset/offset estimates across
approaches}\label{comparing-the-onsetoffset-estimates-across-approaches}

We then compared the ability of the BGAM to accurately estimate the
onset and offset of the ERP difference to other widely-used methods.
First, we conducted mass univariate t-tests (thus treating each time
point independently) and identified the onset and offset of the ERP
difference as the first and last values crossing an arbitrary
significance threshold (\(\alpha = 0.05\)). We then followed the same
approach but after applying different forms of multiplicity correction
to the \(p\)-values. We compared two methods that control the FDR (i.e.,
\texttt{BH95}, \citeproc{ref-benjamini1995}{Benjamini \& Hochberg,
1995}; and \texttt{BY01}, \citeproc{ref-benjamini2001}{Benjamini \&
Yekutieli, 2001}), one method that controls the FWER (i.e.,
Holm--Bonferroni method, \citeproc{ref-holm1979}{Holm, 1979}), and two
cluster-based permutation methods (permutation with a single
cluster-forming threshold and threshold-free cluster enhancement,
\texttt{TFCE}, \citeproc{ref-smith2009}{S. Smith \& Nichols, 2009}). The
\texttt{BH95}, \texttt{BY01}, and \texttt{Holm} corrections were applied
to the \(p\)-values using the \texttt{p.adjust()} function in
\texttt{R}. The cluster-based inference was implemented using a
cluster-sum statistic of \(t\)-values, as implemented in
\texttt{MNE-Python} (\citeproc{ref-gramfort2013}{Gramfort, 2013}),
called via the \texttt{R} package \texttt{reticulate} v 1.46.0
(\citeproc{ref-reticulate}{Ushey et al., 2024}).

We also compared these estimates to the onset and offset estimated using
the binary segmentation algorithm, as implemented in the \texttt{R}
package \texttt{changepoint} v 2.3 (\citeproc{ref-changepoint}{Killick
et al., 2022}), and applied directly to the squared \(t\)-values
(\citeproc{ref-rousselet_using_2025}{Rousselet, 2025}). In contrast to
the participant-level application evaluated by Rousselet
(\citeproc{ref-rousselet_using_2025}{2025}), we applied this procedure
to the group-level statistic, and allowed the number of change points to
be selected automatically. We therefore treat it as a related change
point comparator rather than a direct replication of Rousselet
(\citeproc{ref-rousselet_using_2025}{2025})'s implementation.

Importantly, these comparisons evaluate the complete proposed workflow,
which combines temporal smoothing, multilevel estimation, and Bayesian
posterior inference, against commonly used implementations of the
alternative procedures. They are therefore not intended to isolate the
independent contribution of smoothing, Bayesian inference, or multilevel
modelling to any observed performance difference.

Figure~\ref{fig-corrections} illustrates the temporal detections

several corrected procedures identify intervals largely contained within
the true effect, whereas the uncorrected and FDR-based point-wise
procedures also yield detections outside its temporal boundaries. This
example is intended to illustrate the behaviour of the different
procedures; their operating characteristics across simulations are

\subsubsection{Simulation study}\label{simulation-study}

To assess the accuracy of group-level temporal localisation, all methods
were evaluated across 250 simulated datasets at each of four SNR levels,
yielding 1,000 simulated datasets in total.

For each simulated dataset and method, the complete set of detected time
intervals was compared with the true effect interval(s). Overall
agreement was quantified using temporal intersection-over-union (IoU;
equivalently, the Jaccard index), defined as the duration of the
intersection between the detected and true interval sets divided by the
duration of their union. IoU therefore jointly penalises both missed
portions of the true effect and detections extending outside the true
effect. Performance was summarised across simulations using the median
IoU, with values closer to 1 indicating more accurate recovery of the
true temporal extent.

Boundary localisation was evaluated separately for each true effect
interval. Within the entire window, all detected intervals were
considered jointly: the estimated onset was defined as the earliest
detected onset and the estimated offset as the latest detected offset.
This deliberately stringent definition penalises fragmented or spurious
detections rather than selecting only the detected interval that best
matches the true effect. Onset and offset errors were calculated as the
estimated minus true boundary, such that negative values indicate
boundaries estimated too early and positive values indicate boundaries
estimated too late. For recovered true clusters, boundary localisation
was summarised across simulations using the mean signed error (bias),
mean absolute error (MAE), root mean squared error (RMSE), and standard
deviation (SD) of the signed errors, all expressed in milliseconds.

These simulations were designed to evaluate repeated-sampling
temporal-recovery performance under fixed, controlled data-generating
scenarios. They were not designed as a simulation-based calibration
study: the generating parameters were not drawn from the fitted model's
prior predictive distribution, and the results should therefore not be
interpreted as evidence of prior or posterior calibration.

\subsection{Results}\label{results}

Across SNRs, BGAM consistently achieved the highest median temporal
overlap with the true effect, with IoU increasing from 0.75 at SNR =
0.25 to 0.92 at SNR = 2 (Figure~\ref{fig-simulation-results}A). Boundary
estimates were also comparatively well centred: BGAM showed a small
positive onset bias and offset bias close to zero across SNRs
(Figure~\ref{fig-simulation-results}B). The change point method yielded
similarly small signed boundary biases, but lower temporal overlap,
indicating that accurate average boundary location did not necessarily
translate into accurate recovery of the full effect interval. In
contrast, raw point-wise testing markedly overextended the detected
effect, with systematically early onsets and late offsets, whereas
several corrected frequentist procedures (particularly Holm, TFCE, FDR
BY01, and cluster mass) tended to show the opposite pattern at lower
SNRs, with delayed onsets and premature offsets consistent with temporal
contraction. As SNR increased, differences in IoU between methods
narrowed, although BGAM retained the highest overlap with the true
temporal extent.

The results are further summarised in
Table~\ref{tbl-simulation-results}. Across all SNR levels, BGAM achieved
the highest median temporal overlap with the true effect, with
intersection-over-union (IoU) increasing from 0.75 at SNR = 0.25 to 0.92
at SNR = 2. This advantage was particularly pronounced at lower SNRs,
whereas differences between methods narrowed as signal strength
increased. BGAM also provided comparatively accurate onset localisation,
with mean absolute error (MAE) decreasing from 20.1 ms at SNR = 0.25 to
7.9 ms at SNR = 2. Its onset estimates showed relatively small
systematic bias, approaching zero at intermediate SNRs. At the highest
SNR, cluster-mass inference achieved marginally lower onset MAE and
RMSE, although the differences were small.

Offset localisation showed a more nuanced pattern. BGAM yielded the
lowest offset MAE at SNRs of 0.25, 0.5, and 1, with offset bias
remaining relatively close to zero across all SNRs. However, offset
estimates were generally more variable than onset estimates, and
cluster-mass inference consistently achieved lower offset RMSE,
suggesting that BGAM was more susceptible to occasional large offset
errors. In contrast, uncorrected point-wise testing systematically
overestimated the temporal extent of the effect, producing substantially
earlier onsets and later offsets. Several multiplicity-corrected
procedures, particularly Holm and TFCE, showed the opposite pattern,
with delayed onsets and premature offsets, especially at lower SNRs. The
change point method produced relatively small average boundary biases
but substantially greater absolute errors and lower temporal overlap,
illustrating that low signed bias does not necessarily imply accurate or
reliable temporal localisation. Overall, these simulations indicate that
BGAM provides particularly accurate recovery of the temporal extent and
onset of the simulated effects, while its advantages are less consistent
for offset localisation.

\section{Application to empirical MEG
data}\label{application-to-empirical-meg-data}

\subsection{Methods}\label{methods-1}

To complement the ground-truth simulations, we applied the methods to an
empirical MEG dataset from 32 participants performing a reading task
(\citeproc{ref-nalborczyk:inprep}{Nalborczyk et al., in preparation}).
Time-resolved multivariate pattern analysis yielded, for each
participant, a decoding-accuracy time course expressed as ROC AUC. To
test whether the group-level average decoding accuracy was above chance
(i.e., 0.5) at each time point, we fitted a BGAMM as introduced
previously with a basis dimension \(k=30\) and retained all time points
exceeding a posterior odds of 10. To better distinguish signal from
noise, we defined a baseline-derived threshold as the maximum absolute
bound for the above-chance effect (AUC - 0.5) during the pre-stimulus
baseline period. Although we chose a basis dimension of \(k=30\), which
seemed appropriate for the present data, this choice should be adapted
according to the properties of the modelled data (e.g., signal-to-noise
ratio, prior low-pass filtering, sampling rate) and should be assessed
by the usual model checking tools (e.g., model comparison, posterior
predictive checking, see \hyperref[apx-basis]{Appendix~C}).

To assess the stability of the temporal detections, we performed 1,000
random split-half repetitions of the 32 participants. For each
repetition, participants were randomly divided into two non-overlapping
groups of 16, and all methods were applied independently to each half.
Temporal reproducibility was quantified using the
intersection-over-union (IoU) between the sets of time points detected
in the two halves, defined as the number of time points detected in both
halves divided by the number detected in either half. Thus, an IoU of 1
indicates identical temporal detections, whereas an IoU of 0 indicates
no temporal overlap. We additionally quantified agreement in temporal
segmentation by recording whether the two halves yielded the same number
of detected intervals. Because the repeated partitions reuse
participants and are therefore not statistically independent, these
resampling distributions were interpreted descriptively as measures of
stability rather than as independent observations for inferential
testing.

\subsection{Results}\label{results-1}

Figure~\ref{fig-onset-offset} shows the group-level average decoding
performance through time together with the temporal intervals identified
by each method. The \texttt{Raw\ p-value} and \texttt{FDR\ BH95}
procedures produced the most temporally extensive and fragmented
detections, including above-chance intervals before stimulus onset and
multiple late detections extending well beyond the main decoding
response. The more conservative multiplicity-corrected and cluster-based
procedures (\texttt{FDR\ BY01}, \texttt{Holm}, \texttt{Cluster\ mass},
and \texttt{TFCE}) identified a broadly similar main period of
above-chance decoding beginning at approximately 50-60 ms, although some
methods additionally yielded shorter, later clusters. In contrast, the
\texttt{Change\ point} and \texttt{BGAM} procedures produced
comparatively compact, contiguous intervals: the change-point interval
extended from approximately +60 to +450 ms, while the BGAM detection
covered a similar early period but endedearlier. Thus, the methods
agreed broadly on the onset of the main decoding effect but differed
substantially in its estimated duration and in the degree of temporal
fragmentation.

Temporal detections were reproducible across random split halves,
although stability varied substantially across methods. BGAM showed the
greatest temporal overlap between halves (median IoU = 0.93), followed
by cluster mass (0.87) and the change-point method (0.75); the remaining
point-wise and multiplicity-corrected procedures yielded lower median
overlap (0.60--0.72) (Figure~\ref{fig-reliability}A). BGAM also showed
the most stable cluster count, with the two halves yielding the same
number of detected intervals in almost all split-half repetitions
(Figure~\ref{fig-reliability}B). Cluster-count agreement was
substantially lower for the other procedures, including cluster-mass
correction. When cluster counts differed between halves, discrepancies
were approximately balanced between splits A and B, providing no
indication of systematic split-specific asymmetry. Overall, BGAM
combined the highest temporal overlap with highly consistent
segmentation across repeated non-overlapping halves of the empirical
sample.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

In brief, our results show that the model-based approach provides strong
temporal localisation performance across both ground-truth simulations
and empirical resampling analyses. In simulations, BGAM consistently
yielded the highest temporal overlap with the true effect across SNRs,
together with comparatively accurate onset localisation and small
systematic boundary biases. Its advantage was less uniform for offset
localisation: offset estimates were more variable, and cluster-mass
correction performed better on some offset-specific error metrics,
particularly RMSE. The empirical split-half analysis provided a
complementary assessment of stability when the ground truth was unknown.
Here, BGAM yielded the highest temporal overlap between independently
analysed halves of the sample (median IoU = 0.93), exceeding cluster
mass (0.87) and the change-point procedure (0.75), while also producing
highly consistent cluster counts across repetitions. Thus, the
simulation and empirical analyses converge in showing strong recovery of
temporal support with BGAM, while also highlighting that performance
should be assessed along several complementary dimensions rather than
reduced to a single onset or offset metric.

As with previous simulation studies (e.g.,
\citeproc{ref-rousselet2008}{Rousselet et al., 2008};
\citeproc{ref-sassenhagen2019}{Sassenhagen \& Draschkow, 2019}), results
inevitably depend on design choices, including the specific
cluster-forming algorithm and threshold (for cluster-based methods), the
signal-to-noise ratio, and the potential degradation of temporal
resolution introduced by preprocessing steps such as low-pass filtering.
Temporal filtering should not be assumed to affect all inferential
procedures identically. Because different methods already impose
different forms of temporal regularisation or aggregation, preprocessing
filters may interact with the subsequent statistical analysis and alter
temporal localisation differently across methods. These results
therefore illustrate that relative performance depends not only on the
inferential framework itself, but also on the interaction between the
procedure, signal characteristics, and preprocessing choices. TFCE, for
example, yielded lower temporal overlap than the traditional
cluster-mass procedure in the present simulations, consistent with
previous observations that different forms of cluster inference can have
distinct localisation properties (e.g.,
\citeproc{ref-rousselet_using_2025}{Rousselet, 2025};
\citeproc{ref-smith2009}{S. Smith \& Nichols, 2009}). Interestingly,
Holm and TFCE produced similar temporal detections in the empirical
dataset despite relying on very different inferential mechanisms.

Beyond these comparison-specific considerations, the BGAM itself relies
on modelling assumptions that require careful evaluation. A critical
requirement for any model-based approach is that the model must
adequately capture the relevant features of the data-generating process.
Misspecified models are likely to produce biased or unreliable
onset/offset estimates. This underscores the importance of thorough
model diagnostics, including posterior predictive checks, fit
assessments, and model comparison (\citeproc{ref-gelman2020}{Gelman et
al., 2020}). An important and related methodological consideration
concerns the selection of model hyperparameters, such as the number of
basis functions and the threshold for posterior odds. Although our
simulations suggest that these parameters influence the precision and
reliability of onset and offset estimates, optimal values may vary
depending on the signal's temporal dynamics and signal-to-noise
characteristics. Future work could explore principled approaches to
hyperparameter tuning, including cross-validation or fully Bayesian
model selection using tools such as leave-one-out cross-validation
(\citeproc{ref-gelman2020}{Gelman et al., 2020}). We provide initial
guidance in \hyperref[apx-basis]{Appendix~C} and advocate for future
development of adaptive heuristics to support flexible yet parsimonious
model specification. It should also be noted that penalised smooths are
not designed to reproduce true discontinuities exactly; abrupt changes
may be temporally smeared, particularly when evidence is weak, and the
resulting boundary error can depend on both regularisation and the
decision rule used to define an effect.

Beyond specification of the mean trajectory, a second modelling
consideration concerns residual temporal dependence. The smooth terms
used here model temporal structure in the conditional mean, but do not
imply that the remaining residuals are temporally independent. If
substantial residual autocorrelation remains, posterior uncertainty may
be miscalibrated even when the mean trajectory is well described. The
simulated data used here contain temporal dependence that is not
represented by an explicit residual-correlation process, yet temporal
recovery remained accurate under the conditions considered.
Nevertheless, we recommend inspecting residual dependence in empirical
applications and considering an explicit autocorrelation structure when
warranted.

Other limitations concern the scope of the present implementation and
simulations. The current approach estimates temporal effects
independently at each sensor (1D temporal data). Extending the current
framework to incorporate additional temporal (see
\hyperref[apx-2D]{Appendix~B}) or spatial dimensions would improve both
sensitivity and interpretability. Such extensions could draw on methods
from spatial epidemiology and geostatistics using either GAMMs or
approximate Gaussian process regression (e.g.,
\citeproc{ref-rasmussen2005}{Rasmussen \& Williams, 2005};
\citeproc{ref-riutort-mayol_practical_2023}{Riutort-Mayol et al.,
2023}), depending on computational feasibility. Moreover, the present
simulations primarily evaluate group-level inference with a multilevel
model and therefore should not be taken as evidence of equivalent
localisation performance when the model is fitted to a single
participant, particularly in low-SNR settings where no
between-participant pooling is available. Hierarchical bootstrap
procedures provide another complementary route to population-level
uncertainty in temporal summaries and may be preferable when the
inferential target is a resampled population distribution of onset or
offset estimates rather than an explicit generative model of the
complete time course (\citeproc{ref-rousselet_using_2025}{Rousselet,
2025}).

Finally, practical adoption also requires consideration of computational
cost. It is important to note that the additional temporal precision and
richer uncertainty quantification provided by the BGAM approach come at
a computational cost. Conventional cluster-based permutation tests are
highly optimised and remain substantially faster, making them
particularly well suited for rapid exploratory analyses and large
simulation studies. In the one-dimensional temporal case, cluster-based
permutation tests completed in approximately 2 minutes per dataset
(10,000 permutations), whereas the corresponding BGAM model required
approximately 30 minutes (4 chains, 5,000 iterations each) on a recent
laptop. Thus, BGAMs are not intended as a computationally equivalent
replacement for permutation-based procedures. Rather, they trade
additional computation time for a model-based estimate of the temporal
structure of the effect, while accounting for group structures in the
data and providing posterior evidence through time and direct estimates
of effect onset and offset. Given that model fitting can be parallelised
and that fitting time remains modest relative to the time invested in
experimental design, data collection, preprocessing, and large-scale
validation analyses, we view this computational cost as acceptable when
the goal is accurate and robust temporal characterisation rather than
only cluster-level significance testing (as noted by
\citeproc{ref-SPENCER2022118908}{Spencer et al., 2022}, in the context
of spatial Bayesian GLMs applied to fMRI data).

To facilitate adoption, we developed the \texttt{neurogam} open-source
\texttt{R} package, which implements the proposed method using
\texttt{brms}. The package integrates seamlessly with
\texttt{MNE-Python} (\citeproc{ref-gramfort2013}{Gramfort, 2013}),
enabling researchers to process M/EEG data in \texttt{Python} and import
them directly into \texttt{R} for model-based inference without
cumbersome data export. This interoperability, described in
\hyperref[apx-package]{Appendix~D}, is designed to encourage broader use
of model-based approaches in cognitive neuroscience.

In conclusion, we introduced a model-based approach for temporally
localised inference in M/EEG data that explicitly represents the
temporal trajectory and between-participant variability. Across
ground-truth simulations, the approach provided the most accurate
overall recovery of the true temporal extent and comparatively accurate
onset localisation across SNRs, although offset estimates were more
variable and conventional procedures performed better on some
offset-specific metrics. In empirical MEG data, BGAM also yielded the
highest split-half temporal overlap and highly consistent temporal
segmentation across repeated participant splits. These converging
results support BGAMs as a flexible and useful complement to
conventional procedures when explicit modelling of temporal structure,
multilevel variation, and uncertainty in temporal localisation are
scientifically important.

\section{Formal specification of the Bayesian additive multilevel
model}\label{apx-math}

\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}
\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}

Let \(y_i\) denote the mean EEG signal for observation \(i\), with known
standard error \(s_i\), participant \(j[i]\), condition contrast
\(c_i\), condition label \(g_i \in \{1,2\}\), and time \(t_i\). The
model can be written as:

\[
\begin{aligned}
y_i &\sim \operatorname{Normal}\left(\mu_i,\sqrt{\sigma^2 + s_i^2}\right) \\[4pt]
\mu_i
&=
\alpha
+ a_{j[i]}
+ \left(\beta + b_{j[i]}\right)c_i
+ f_{g_i}(t_i)
+ h_{g_i,j[i]}(t_i) \\[4pt]
\begin{bmatrix}
a_j \\
b_j
\end{bmatrix}
&\sim \operatorname{MVNormal}
\left(
\begin{bmatrix}
0 \\
0
\end{bmatrix},
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
\right) \\[4pt]
\boldsymbol{\Sigma} &= \mathbf{S}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{S} \\[4pt]
\mathbf{S} &=
\begin{bmatrix}
\sigma_a & 0 \\
0 & \sigma_b
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Here, \(\alpha\) is the population-level intercept, \(\beta\) is the
population-level condition effect, and \(a_j\) and \(b_j\) are
participant-level deviations in intercept and condition effect (i.e.,
varying intercept and slope). The function \(f_g(t)\) represents the
population-level temporal trajectory for condition \(g\), whereas
\(h_{g,j}(t)\) represents the participant-specific smooth deviation from
that population trajectory. The residual scale combines the estimated
residual standard deviation \(\sigma\) with the known standard error
\(s_i\) of each participant-level mean.

The population-level condition-specific smooths can be represented using
basis functions as

\[
f_g(t_i)
=
\sum_{m=1}^{M}
w_{gm} B_m(t_i),
\]

where \(B_m(t_i)\) are spline basis functions and \(w_{gm}\) are
condition-specific spline coefficients. Participant-specific smooth
deviations can analogously be written as

\[
h_{g,j}(t_i)
=
\sum_{m=1}^{M_j}
u_{gjm} B^{(j)}_m(t_i),
\]

where \(u_{gjm}\) are participant- and condition-specific spline
coefficients. Their magnitude and smoothness are controlled by
penalisation parameters estimated hierarchically from the data. The
priors follow the default \texttt{brms} specification:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\alpha &\sim \operatorname{Student}\text{-}t(3,0,2.5) \\[4pt]
\beta &\sim \operatorname{flat} \\[4pt]
\sigma &\sim \operatorname{Student}\text{-}t^+(3,0,2.5) \\[4pt]
\sigma_a, \sigma_b &\sim \operatorname{Student}\text{-}t^+(3,0,2.5) \\[4pt]
\sigma_{\text{smooth, pop}} &\sim \operatorname{Student}\text{-}t^+(3,0,2.5) \\[4pt]
\sigma_{\text{smooth, participant}} &\sim \operatorname{Student}\text{-}t^+(3,0,2.5) \\[4pt]
\mathbf{R} &\sim \operatorname{LKJ}(1).
\end{aligned}
\]

The superscript \(+\) indicates truncation to positive values. The
correlation matrix \(\mathbf{R}\) describes the correlation between
participant-level intercepts and condition effects.

\section{Application to 2D time-resolved decoding results
(cross-temporal generalisation)}\label{apx-2D}

\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}
\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}

We conducted a cross-temporal generalisation analysis of the decoding
data from Nalborczyk et al. (\citeproc{ref-nalborczyk:inprep}{in
preparation}), in which we assessed the performance of classifiers
trained and tested at various time points of the trial
(\citeproc{ref-king2014}{King \& Dehaene, 2014}). This analysis was
performed at the participant level, resulting in a 2D matrix where each
element contains the decoding accuracy (ROC AUC) of a classifier trained
at time point \(\text{training}_{i}\) and tested at time point
\(\text{testing}_{j}\) for each participant (Figure~\ref{fig-timegen}).

\begin{figure}[!htb]

\caption{\label{fig-timegen}Group-level average cross-temporal
generalisation matrix of decoding performance (data from
\citeproc{ref-nalborczyk:inprep}{Nalborczyk et al., in preparation}).}

\centering{

\includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth,height=\textheight,keepaspectratio]{manuscript_files/figure-pdf/fig-timegen-1.png}

}

\end{figure}%

\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}
\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}

To model cross-temporal generalisation matrices of decoding performance,
we extended our initial BGAM to take into account the bivariate temporal
distribution of AUC values, thus producing naturally smoothed estimates
(time courses) of AUC values and posterior odds. This model can be
written as follows:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{AUC}_{i} &\sim \mathrm{Beta} \left(\mu_{i}, \phi\right)\\
\operatorname{logit} \left(\mu_{i} \right) &= \alpha + f \left(\text{train}_{i}, \text{test}_{i} \right)\\
f \left(\text{train}_{i}, \text{test}_{i} \right) &\equiv \sum_{k=1}^K b_{k} B_{k}\left(\text{train}_{i}, \text{test}_{i} \right)
\end{aligned}
\]

\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}
\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}

where AUC values are assumed to follow a \(\mathrm{Beta}\) distribution,
parametrised by a mean \(\mu_{i}\) and a precision parameter \(\phi\).
The mean \(\mu_{i}\) is linked to the predictors through a logit link
function. The smooth function \(f(\text{train}_{i}, \text{test}_{i})\)
represents a two-dimensional surface defined over training and testing
times, which captures how decoding performance varies across the
temporal generalisation matrix. This surface is approximated by a linear
combination of \(K\) basis functions \(B_{k}(\cdot, \cdot)\), each
weighted by a coefficient \(b_{k}\). The basis functions are constructed
using a tensor product of univariate splines (here thin-plate splines)
applied to the training and testing time dimensions
(\citeproc{ref-wood2003}{Wood, 2003}, \citeproc{ref-wood2017}{2017}).

\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}
\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}

We fitted this model using \texttt{brms} and the \texttt{t2()} tensor
product smooth constructor with full penalties
(\citeproc{ref-pedersen_hierarchical_2019}{E. J. Pedersen et al., 2019};
\citeproc{ref-wood2017}{Wood, 2017}). We ran four MCMCs to approximate
the posterior distribution, including each 3,000 iterations and a warmup
of 1,000 iterations, yielding a total of 8,000 posterior samples to be
used for inference.

\section{How to choose the GAM basis dimension?}\label{apx-basis}

\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}
\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}

There is no universal recommendation for choosing the optimal value of
\texttt{k}, as it depends on several factors, including the sampling
rate, preprocessing steps (e.g., signal-to-noise ratio, low-pass
filtering), and the underlying neural dynamics of the phenomenon under
investigation. A practical strategy is to choose \(k\) sufficiently
large that the basis dimension does not artificially constrain the
smooth, and then assess its adequacy using posterior predictive checks
and sensitivity analyses. Alternatively, models with different values of
\(k\) can be compared using predictive criteria such as LOO. We
illustrate this approach below.

\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}
\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}

Figure~\ref{fig-choose-k} presents the posterior predictions and two
forms of posterior predictive checks (PPCs) for each GAM fit using
different numbers of basis functions (\(k \in \{5, 10, 20, 40, 80\}\)).
With the exception of the \(k=5\) model, all other fits yield
satisfactory PPCs, indicating that the predicted data closely resemble
the empirical observations. However, model comparison using the
leave-one-out information criterion (LOOIC), as summarised in
Table~\ref{tbl-k-results}, identifies the \(k=40\) model as the
best-performing one in terms of LOOIC, closely followed by the \(k=80\)
model. Future simulation studies could further investigate how such
model selection criteria relate to the precision of onset and offset
estimates.
